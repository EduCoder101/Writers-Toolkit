Learning intention and success criteria ▼

Learning intention

We are learning to use a dependent clause that starts with when, while, although or because, and to try it at the start or the end of a sentence to build tension.

Success criteria

  • I can spot the main clause and the dependent clause in a complex sentence.
  • I can choose a tension word that makes sense, and put a comma after a dependent clause that comes first.
  • I can use dependent clauses at least twice in my own narrative writing.
  • I can explain how moving the dependent clause changes the tension for the reader.

The trick: make your reader wait

The lights flickered.Main clause: makes sense on its own She reached for the handle.Dependent clause: can't stand alone
Two short sentences. Read them aloud. They feel a bit choppy.

The reader has to wait through "As she reached for the handle..." before the main action arrives. Wait for it... the lights flickered! That waiting is tension.

Tension words start a dependent clause: whenwhileasbecausealthough

Grammar name: these are subordinating conjunctions.

A complex sentence has a main clause that makes sense on its own and a dependent clause that needs the main clause.

Part B: flip them in your book

Each partner writes in their own book. Rewrite each sentence with the dependent clause first, add the comma, then draw a ring around it. Write it first, then press Check.

  1. 1

    Zara held her breath while the footsteps came closer.

    While the footsteps came closer, Zara held her breath.

  2. 2

    Ava kept climbing although the ladder wobbled.

    Although the ladder wobbled, Ava kept climbing.Ava keeps her capital letter because it is a name.

  3. 3

    Nobody moved because the floorboards would creak.

    Because the floorboards would creak, nobody moved.Nobody loses its capital letter now that it is in the middle of the sentence.

  4. 4

    Grace froze when the handle began to turn.

    When the handle began to turn, Grace froze.Talk: which version is scarier? Some writers would save “the handle began to turn” for the end. Both are correct!

Challenge: join, then flip

Join the two sentences with a tension word. Put the dependent clause first.

  1. A

    The lights went out. Mia grabbed her torch.

    One way: When the lights went out, Mia grabbed her torch.As or because would work too. Did you remember the comma?

  2. B

    Ruby was frightened. She opened the cupboard.

    One way: Although Ruby was frightened, she opened the cupboard.Although shows Ruby is brave even though she is scared.

Fast finishers

Rewrite the passage

The choppy version
The Last One Out

1Isla stayed back in the library. 2Everyone else had gone home. 3The lights hummed. 4She packed her bag. 5Something thumped onto the floor. 6A book had fallen off a shelf. 7Nobody was there. 8Isla was scared. 9She walked towards the shelf. 10The door slammed shut behind her.

Ten short sentences. Every one starts the same way, so the tension never builds.

In your book

Rewrite the passage so the reader holds their breath. Join sentences with tension words and move the clauses around. Keep every event, in the same order.

Learn this

Rewriting is where tension is built. Join choppy sentences, then move each clause until the scariest part lands in the best place.

Need a hint? Sentences that could join
1 and 23 and 44 and 58 and 99 and 10

Sentence 7 is short and spooky. You could keep it short for punch. Can you show that Isla was scared, instead of telling us?

Finished? See a model
Although everyone else had gone home, Isla stayed back in the library. While the lights hummed above her, she packed her bag. She was zipping it up when something thumped onto the floor. A book had fallen off a shelf. Nobody was there. Although her heart was pounding, Isla walked towards the shelf. As she reached it, the door slammed shut behind her.
  1. Four dependent clauses come first, each followed by a comma.
  2. One comes last: when something thumped onto the floor. The surprise lands at the end of the sentence.
  3. Two sentences stay short. Mixing long and short sentences adds punch.
  4. "Isla was scared" became "her heart was pounding". Show, don't tell!
  5. The passage ends on the action: the door slams.

Go Deeper: does it always go first?

Specialising

Read both sentences aloud. Which one gives you more of a shock?

AWhen someone tapped her on the shoulder, Ruby froze.
BRuby froze when someone tapped her on the shoulder.

Find the scariest words in each sentence. Are they at the start or the end?

A writer's thinking

My first thought was A, because we practised putting the dependent clause first. Then I read them both aloud.

In A, the tap on the shoulder comes first, so by the time I read "Ruby froze", I already know what happened. In B, I read "Ruby froze" and wonder why. The tap on the shoulder lands right at the end, like a jump scare.

So B gives me more of a shock, even though its dependent clause comes last.

Writer's thinking: You are specialising. That means you are trying out examples to gather evidence before you make a rule.
Conjecturing

Always, sometimes or never? "Putting the dependent clause first always builds more tension."

Use Ruby's sentences and one sentence from Flip it as your evidence.

A writer's thinking

I started by saying always, because every sentence in Flip it got tenser when I flipped it. Then Ruby's sentence broke my rule.

When the scary part is in the main clause, like "the door slowly creaked open", putting the dependent clause first makes us wait for it. When the scary part is in the dependent clause, like "someone tapped her on the shoulder", it works best at the end.

So my answer is sometimes.

Writer's thinking: You are conjecturing. That means you are making a claim from your evidence, knowing it might still need testing.
Generalising

Make a rule for where the scariest part of a sentence should go. Test it: write one sentence where the dependent clause belongs at the start, and one where it belongs at the end.

Swap with your partner. Does your rule work for their sentences too?

A writer's thinking

My first rule was "the dependent clause always goes first", but that made Ruby's sentence weaker, so I threw it out.

My new rule: save the scariest part for the end of the sentence. If the scary part is the main clause, put the dependent clause first. If the scary part is the dependent clause, put it last.

Test 1: "Although she was shaking, Ava reached for the handle." The action is in the main clause, so the dependent clause goes first.

Test 2: "Ava gasped as the handle began to turn." The surprise is in the dependent clause, so it goes last. My rule works for both.

Writer's thinking: You are generalising. That means you are making a rule that works every time, not just for one example.
