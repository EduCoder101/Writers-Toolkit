Paint the NounExpanded noun groups and appositives

We are learning to expand a noun with specific detail and add an appositive: a noun group that renames or explains another noun.

20 minutesRefresherOne quick writing burst
straight line = expanded noun groupwavy line = appositive

Zara, the youngest explorer, entered the narrow, shadowy tunnel.

Success criteria
I can spot an expanded noun group and an appositive in a sentence.
I can use both accurately in my own narrative, with commas around my appositive.
I can explain how each one helps the reader picture the story.

Two tools you already own

Learn this: two checks for an appositive
1 · Equals test
Put = between the noun and the noun group straight after it: Zara = the youngest explorer. Same person or thing? It is an appositive.
2 · Lift it out
Take the appositive out with its commas: Zara entered the tunnel. Still a sentence? Your commas are in the right place.
Watch me build it
StartA bare sentence.

The explorer entered.

RenameAdd an appositive.

Zara, the youngest explorer, entered.

Thinking aloud: the reader does not know who this is. I give her a name, and keep the youngest explorer right beside it to explain her. Now we know she is young, and that there are other explorers.

ExpandPaint the noun.

Zara, the youngest explorer, entered the narrow, shadowy tunnel.

Thinking aloud: entered what? I add the noun tunnel, then pack detail around it. Narrow makes it tight. Shadowy makes it dark. The reader starts to worry for her.

Talk to your partnerWhat does the reader know now that they did not know from The explorer entered?

This is a refresher

Students met appositives in Writing with Appositives. Ask before you tell: show each tool card and get the class to name it, explain it and give an example before you read the card.

The one idea to land

An appositive and an expanded noun group can look identical. The difference is the job, not the shape. Expanded noun group: one name, more detail. Appositive: a second name for the same thing, straight after the first. The equals test is the quickest way for students to tell them apart. Give the "Spot the difference" question a full partner turn before you show the name tags.

A tidy-up from last time

The word bank in the earlier lesson included who…, with… and known for… starters. Those make good sentences, but they are not appositives (who loved to read is a relative clause). The equals test sorts them out: Mia = who loved to read makes no sense, so it is not an appositive. Quick check 4 (a tiny spider with hairy legs) is there for the same reason: with… adds detail to one noun, so it belongs to the expanded noun group. Today, keep appositives to noun groups, as the program defines them. If a student writes Zara, who was the youngest, entered…, praise the sentence and ask them to turn the middle into a noun group: Zara, the youngest explorer, entered…

If they ask
  • Is the appositive a noun group too? Yes. Every appositive is a noun group, and often an expanded one: the guardian of the old house has detail after its noun. What makes it an appositive is its job: it comes straight after another noun and is a second name for the same thing. That is why it gets the wavy line.
  • Why a comma in narrow, shadowy? Put a comma between two describing words when you could say "and" between them: narrow and shadowy. The heavy oak door has no comma, because "heavy and oak door" sounds wrong.

Quick voteWhich version gives the reader the strongest picture? Point to the one word doing the most work.
Running the we do
  • For each card, take one noun group and one appositive from the class before you press "Show a version". Write the best class idea on the board beside the bare sentence.
  • The revealed sentence is one writer's choice, not the answer. If the class version is stronger, say so.
  • After each reveal, ask "What does the reader know now?" before reading the answer under it.
  • Fiercely and creaked are bonus detail in the verbs. Praise them if noticed, but keep the focus on the nouns.
4 · You write5 minIn your book

Something mysterious

Introduce a mysterious character or creature in two or three connected sentences. Use the tools at least twice: at least one expanded noun group and at least one appositive.

Pick a picture, or use your own idea.

A creature

Under the jetty

  • What is it? What does its skin, scales or fur look like?
  • What second name could it have? The last of something? The guardian of something?
  • What is it watching?
Need a starter?

Beneath the slimy wooden jetty, something stirred. The creature, the last ________ of ________, …

A character

The lighthouse window

  • Who is at the window? What is their name?
  • What is their job, or what are they the keeper of?
  • What are they holding? What is the room like?
Need a starter?

High in the lighthouse, a single window glowed. Old Morrow, the ________ of ________, …

Zara's story

The end of the tunnel

  • What is waiting for Zara at the end of the tunnel?
  • What could it be called? What is it the only one of?
  • What does Zara notice first: eyes, horns, a glint?
Need a starter?

At the end of the tunnel, Zara froze. In front of her crouched a ________, ________ creature, the ________ …

Running the burst
  • Students write in their books. Keep the pictures projected. Top writers go straight in with their own idea; point support students to a picture and its starter.
  • Start the timer once most pencils are moving. Walk the room looking for commas around the appositive: that is the most common wobble.
  • "Two or three connected sentences" means one character or creature across all of them. If a student writes three unrelated sentences, ask "Who is your second sentence about?"
  • With 30 seconds left, say "last sentence".

Swap and check

Swap books with your partner. First you are the detective, then the coach.

  1. 1In pencil, rule a straight line under each expanded noun group and draw a wavy line under each appositive.
  2. 2Check the commas around each appositive. Is there one before it and one after it?

  4. 4Swap back. Rewrite one sentence, making the change.

Coach stems
  • Your ____ is still a blur. Which ____ is it?
  • What could be a second name for ____?
  • Could ____ be more specific than ____?
  • You need a comma after ____.
Keep it tight
  • Pencil only, and one suggestion, not five. The writer chooses whether to take it.
  • If a partner finds no appositive, that is the improvement: "What could be a second name for ____?"
  • While pairs work, find two students with a clear before and after for the exit.

This week

Keep both tools switched on. In your writing and your spelling sentences this week, paint at least one noun every time you write.

For the teacher

Look for: an appositive that is a noun group (not a who… clause), commas on both sides, and detail that is specific rather than piled on. "The big, huge, giant dog" is three words doing one job; ask which one word paints it best. A student who explains what the reader now knows has met the third success criterion.
