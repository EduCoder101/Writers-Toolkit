Learning Intention

We are learning to edit a draft so it is clearer, more correct and more interesting to read.

Success Criteria

  • I can find and fix grammar, punctuation and dialogue mistakes.
  • I can swap weak words and fix sentences that are too short or too long.
  • I can change telling into showing.
  • I can explain why my best edit makes the writing better.
Before the lesson
  • Print the stories PDF. Each story is one double-spaced page with a margin for codes. Students choose, so print roughly a third of a class set of each story, plus a few spares.
  • Every student needs a red pen (or red pencil) and their English book.
  • Laptops for card 12, Type up and compare. Students open this page and click Type up and compare in the sidebar, or add #compare to the end of the link.
Vocabulary

Edit, draft, precise, tense, speech marks, apostrophe.

Suggested timing (30 minutes)

What would you change: 2 · Six things to hunt for: 1 · Six modelling cards: 7 · Choose your story: 1 · Red pen time: 12 · Type up and compare: 5 · My best edit: 2

Keep in mind

A messy page is a good page. Every crossing-out shows thinking. Editing is not only fixing mistakes; it is making better choices.

Every writer edits
Writers never get it right the first time. Every book you have read was edited many times before it was printed.
Learn this
Editing means making better choices, not just fixing mistakes.
Run it
  • Read the draft aloud. Partners find two things to change before you reveal anything.
  • Take three or four ideas, then click Red pen it. The edits appear one at a time, so you can name each one as it lands.
  • Ask: which change made the biggest difference? Most students pick “I bit my lip” or “shrieked”. That is the point: the best edits are choices, not corrections.

Six things editors hunt for

In your book
Under your title, write the six codes down the page: WC G S D SDT P. Leave a line next to each one.
WC
Word choice
went, got, said, nice, very, really
went bounded
G
Grammar
we was, me and Ava, could of, mixed-up tense
we was we were
S
Sentences
lots of short sentences in a row, long “and then” sentences
join with when or although
D
Dialogue
missing speech marks, punctuation outside them, two speakers on one line
new speaker, new line
SDT
Show, don't tell
was scared, was happy, was angry
She was scared. Her hands shook.
P
Punctuation
missing capitals, apostrophes and commas in lists
emmas Emma’s
Run it
  • Keep this quick. Students copy the six codes now; they add a note next to each code on the next six cards.
  • Tell them the codes will go in the margin of their story sheet, next to every edit they make.

About the stories
  • Open the stories PDF to print more copies.

Red pen time

On your sheet
  1. Read your story once, pen down.
  2. Red pen it: cross out, add with ^, write your change above.
  3. Write a code in the margin next to every edit.
  4. Hunt for every kind. Your story hides at least 2 of each.
Stuck?
  • Hunt for one code at a time. Read once just for P, then again just for D.
  • Circle these words: went, got, said, really, very.
  • Find every was + a feeling. That is telling.
  • Whisper it. Where do you run out of breath? That sentence is too long.
Go further

Your story breaks one of our opening rules. Find it and rewrite the first sentence as a sizzling start.

Then fix the last sentence so the ending is not just “a good day”.

Compare with an editor
What did you find that the editor missed? What did the editor change that you did not? There is more than one good way to edit.
Two ways to type up
  • Start with the first draft copies the original into the box so students edit it in place. This is quickest and keeps the comparison clean.
  • Typing from scratch takes longer but is good practice. Any new typos will also show up as changes, which is worth talking about.
Good to know
  • Each student's typing is kept in their own browser, so they can come back to it later on the same laptop. Nothing is sent anywhere.
  • On the board, open this card on your computer and paste in a student's version to compare it with the class.
  • The editor's version is one possible edit, not the answer. Ask students to defend a change they made that the editor did not.

Assessment check

Look for edits across all six codes, not just punctuation, and a reason that talks about the reader (“it shows she is nervous”, “it is easier to follow”).
