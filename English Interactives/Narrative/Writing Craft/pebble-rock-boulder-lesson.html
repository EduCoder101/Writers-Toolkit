Learning Intention

We are learning that stories need problems, and that tension builds when a small problem grows into a bigger one.

Success Criteria

  • I can explain why a story needs problems.
  • I can spot the problems growing in stories I know.
  • I can check a problem ladder with the worry test and the because test.
  • I can plan a pebble and a rock for my own story that connect to my sizzling start.
Before the lesson
  • Students need their books, with their sizzling start from Lesson 1 and their backfill from Lesson 2.
  • One mini whiteboard and marker per group of 3 to 4. Decide groups and give each group a story idea (two groups per idea works well).
  • Optional props for card 7: a real pebble and a fist-sized rock to hold up.
  • Have your copies of The Explorer and Mrs Frisby and the Rats of NIMH handy. Cards 3 and 4 retell the moments in our own words, with chapter pointers so you can read the real pages aloud.
  • Print the Problem Ladders sheet double-sided, one per student: Story Starter Ladders on the front, blank My Problem Ladders on the back. Students glue it into their books after the lesson and keep it for Lessons 4 to 7.
Suggested timing (40 minutes)

Hook and the three stories: 6 · Rules, names and Lily's ladder: 7 · Wobbly or strong: 3 · Group challenge and share: 8 · Story starter ladders and because test: 9 · Your own ladder: 5 · Exit slip: 2

The flow is groups first (the four unit topics), then on their own with fresh stories (the story starters), then their own story. If time runs short, open Lesson 4 with the blank ladders.

Hook
Talk

Is this a story?

Ella went to the beach. She swam and built a sandcastle. She had a lovely day.The end.
Talk to your partner
Thumbs up if you would read a whole book about Ella's day. Thumbs down if you would not. Why not? What is missing?
The big idea
Nothing goes wrong, so there is nothing to worry about. Readers keep reading because they want to know: will the character be OK? Problems create that worry. Writers call it tension.
In your book
Write the date and the title Pebble, Rock, Boulder. Underneath, copy: Problems make the reader worry. That worry is called tension. Tension keeps the reader reading.
Say and ask

Read Ella's story aloud in your most cheerful voice. Ask: "Is that a story? Would you read a whole book of it?" Steer to: nothing goes wrong, so there is nothing to worry about and no reason to keep reading.

Take two or three quick ideas for what could go wrong for Ella, then move on. Ella is the first story on the story starter sheet later, so this warms them up.

Pebble, rock, boulder

Writers have names for problem 1, 2 and 3. Imagine you are out on a long walk.

Pebble
A pebble in your shoe. Annoying, but you can stop and tip it out.
A small problem. Annoying, but fixable.
Rock

A bigger problem that grows out of the pebble.

In your book
Draw a pebble, a rock and a boulder, smallest to biggest. Label them: pebble = small problem, annoying but fixable rock = bigger problem that grows out of the pebble boulder = the biggest problem
With props

Hold up a real pebble: "A pebble in your shoe is annoying, but you can fix it." Hold up the rock: "A rock on the path is harder. You might trip." Reveal the boulder: "A boulder blocks the whole way. We'll get to that next lesson."

Link back: in the three stories, problem 1 was the pebble and problem 2 was the rock.

Meet Lily

This is my story. We will follow it for the whole unit, while you write yours.

It is the last night of Year 5 camp. Lily is walking back from the toilet block on her own.

Look at Lily's worry. Keep it in mind. You will see why in a moment.

My pebble

Her torch flickers, then dies.

Small and annoying. She can still just see the path, so she can fix it: keep walking, carefully.

Why did I choose a torch? Because Lily's worry is the dark. A good pebble pokes at your character's worry.

My first try at a rock

At dinner, Lily spilled her drink all over the table.

Talk to your partner. Run the two tests.

?
Worry testIs Lily more worried now than when her torch died?
?
Because test"Her torch died, and because of that, she spilled her drink at dinner." Does that make sense?

Wobbly!

Worry testA spilled drink is much smaller than being alone in the dark.
Because testDinner was hours ago. It happened before the torch died, so the torch cannot have caused it.

It is smaller, it is not connected, and it is in the wrong order. How could we fix it? Talk to your partner.

A stronger rock

Because it is so dark, she takes the wrong track. When she looks up, the cabins have gone. She is lost.
Worry testLost, alone, in the dark. Much worse than a flat torch.
Because test"Her torch died, and because of that, she took the wrong track and got lost." Yes!

Lily's ladder so far

Notice Lily's feelings grow too: annoyed, then worried. When the feelings grow, the tension grows.

Go further
  • Poke at the worry: choose problems that hit what your character fears most, like Lily's torch.
  • Make it matter: give your character a reason this problem matters to them (they promised Mum, it is their last chance to make the team).
  • My pebble is a small problem: annoying, but fixable.
  • My rock is harder. My character is more worried. (Worry test)
  • My rock happens because of my pebble. (Because test)
  • I wrote how my character feels on both rungs.

Assessment check

Collect books or walk the room. Look for: a clear pebble and a bigger rock; a cause-and-effect link between them (the because test); problems that suit the chosen topic; a feeling on each rung.

Note students whose rock is not connected, or not bigger than the pebble, and conference with them before Lesson 4. The story starter side shows who can use the idea on a brand new story; question 2 shows who can explain it.
