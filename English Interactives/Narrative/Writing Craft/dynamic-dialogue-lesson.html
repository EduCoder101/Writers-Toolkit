Learning Intention

We are learning to use dynamic dialogue to make our scenes more dramatic, reveal our characters, and move the plot along.

Success Criteria

  • I can explain what dialogue is and the purposes it serves in a story.
  • I can punctuate dialogue correctly using quotation marks, commas, and a new paragraph for each speaker.
  • I can use a verb other than "said" to show how something is spoken.
  • I can add description of what is happening while a character speaks, to bring the scene to life.
  • I can rewrite a "telling" passage as a scene of dynamic dialogue.

But Sir? What on earth do you mean by dynamic dialogue?

Read both versions of this scene. Which one pulls you into the chaos?

Version A — telling
I walked into school and got there late. Everyone was in class. I opened the door and there were kids everywhere. Boys were climbing out of the window and there were paper planes all over the place. My best friend Anna was holding one of the rats from the science lab and the rest were running all over the room. Anna told me there were no teachers, none at all.
Version B — with dialogue
I was late to school again, everyone was in class. I opened the door to the classroom at full speed.

"Duck!" yelled Matt, as a huge paper aeroplane came diving right at my head.

"Come on." Jack was halfway out of the window, two boys right behind him.

My best friend Anna was holding one of the rats from the science lab, the rest running everywhere.

"What's happening?" I asked her.

"There's no teachers," she cried. "None at all!"
Discussion
Both versions tell the same story, but Version B puts us inside the chaos. The dialogue makes the scene dynamic — it moves, it crackles, it lets us hear the panic. By the end of this lesson, you will write scenes like this.

What is Dialogue?

Have a think. Write down your own definition. If you're struggling, chat to the person next to you.

Definition
Dialogue is a discussion between two or more people — in the real world, or as a feature of a book, play, or film.
Compare
What then is a monologue? A long speech by one person — either inside a conversation, or by an actor in a play or film.
Tip: Change or improve your own definition now that you've seen these. Writers borrow good wording all the time.

Why Use Dialogue?

Dialogue does three main things in a story.

⚡
Make it dynamic
Turn flat description into moments of drama and tension.
👻
Reveal character
Show who your characters are, how they feel, what they think.
➡️
Move the plot
Give the reader information without long blocks of narration.
Big idea: Used well, dialogue does more than carry words. It tells us what type of people your characters are, how they're feeling, and what they're doing while they speak. That makes for richer stories — both to write and to read.

The Rules of Dialogue

Before we get creative, let's lock in the basics.

1
Always use quotation marks around the words a character actually says.
2
Begin a new paragraph each time a different person speaks.
3
Full stops and commas go inside the quotation marks, not outside.
4
Make sure your characters speak like real people, not robots.

Punctuation Puzzle

Here is some dialogue with no quotation marks, no commas, no paragraph breaks. Have a go at fixing it in your book, then tap to reveal the corrected version.

Broken
Hi said George. John answered him sharply hello, what can I do for you? Oh nothing said George

"Hi," said George.

John answered him sharply, "Hello, what can I do for you?"

"Oh nothing," said George.

Notice
Three speakers? No — just two, but each new turn gets a new paragraph. Commas slip inside the quotation marks. Question marks and exclamation marks do the same job.

Purpose 1: Make Your Scene Dynamic

Use dialogue to push the story forward through drama or tension.

Telling
Alan saw something out of the corner of his eye and told Dave. Dave said he had seen it too and they set off.
Showing with dialogue
"Did you see that?" shouted Alan as something in the corner of his eye caught his attention.

"I saw it too," agreed Dave hastily. "Let's head over there and find out what's going on."

They both started running.

Jeremy screwed up his face when he didn't understand the instruction. He muttered something under his breath and went to work.
Sally went to the door, opened it, and shouted at the man standing on the doorstep. He screamed and ran away.
Mark said he understood his master's instructions and turned away to cast his first spell. It didn't go to plan.
Model (scene 1)
"What does this even mean?" Jeremy muttered, screwing up his face.
"Just... give it a go," whispered Tom, glancing at the teacher.
"Fine." Jeremy snatched the worksheet from the desk and started scribbling.

Purpose 2: Reveal Your Characters

Use dialogue to show who characters are, what they think, and what they feel.

Telling
Sally told her mum that she wished she had never run away. Her mum gave her a hug and said she was happy that Sally had come home.
Showing with dialogue
Sally wept as she whispered in a sad voice, "I wish I had never run away."

Her mum put her arms around her, holding her close. "Don't worry, my darling. We're just happy you came home."

Your turn. Pick one and rewrite using dialogue:

Macey wondered what would happen if she and Amy mixed the two chemicals. When Amy encouraged her to try, Macey did so hesitatingly.
John asked his dad for some advice on how to win the race. His dad said some wise words and John felt excited for the day ahead.
Model (scene 1)
"What do you reckon would happen if we mixed them?" Macey asked, her finger hovering over the green bottle.
"Only one way to find out," grinned Amy. "Go on."
"Okay... but if Mrs Patel finds out, this was your idea." Macey unscrewed the lid, hand trembling.

Purpose 3: Move the Plot Along

Use dialogue to deliver information — without long, dry narration.

Telling
Ford explained to Mandy why he had been gone for so long. He told her how he had been stuck on Earth for fifteen years. He had only meant to go for a three week holiday. Mandy wanted to know how he had travelled to Earth in the first place.
Showing with dialogue
"Unfortunately I got stuck on Earth for rather longer than I intended," said Ford. "I came for a three week holiday and got stuck for fifteen years!"

"But how did you get there in the first place?" asked Mandy.

Your turn. Rewrite this scene using dialogue:

Andrew said he knew how hard it had been for Rocky to admit it was his fault. He told Rocky that it was going to be ok and he would be forgiven. Rocky looked at him and quietly replied that he was grateful for Andrew's kindness.
Model response
"That took guts, Rocky," said Andrew, his voice softer than usual. "Admitting it was your fault — not many people would have."
"I know." Rocky stared at the carpet.
"You'll be alright. We can fix this." Andrew sat down beside him. "Everyone's going to forgive you."
Rocky looked up, his eyes wet. "Thanks," he whispered. "I really mean it."

Dialogue Is More Than the Talking

In our stories, dialogue is not just the words inside the quotation marks. Look back at this example. What else is happening?

Sally wept as she whispered in a sad voice "I wish I had never run away."

Her mum put her arms around her holding her close, before saying "Don't worry my darling, we're just happy you came home."
Element 1 — What is said
The words inside the quotation marks. The actual content of the conversation.
Element 2 — How it is said
The verb of speaking, plus any adverb. Whispered, shouted, in a sad voice, hastily, firmly.
Element 3 — What is happening
The action around the words. Sally wept. Her mum put her arms around her. These actions sit beside the dialogue and bring it to life.

How It Is Said

Same words. Very different feeling. Tap each sentence to see what changes.

"If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now."
Flat. The reader has no clue how this is delivered — angrily, quietly, sadly?
Alice whispered, "If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now."
A whisper turns this into a held-back accusation. Quiet. Bitter. Just between the two of them.
Alice stammered, "If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now."
Now she's nervous, maybe afraid of her own words. The accusation barely escapes her mouth.
Alice exclaimed, "If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now!"
Loud, sharp, no filter. The exclamation mark amplifies it.

Your turn. Add a "how" to each of these lines — the speaker, the verb, and an adverb or phrase:

"I'm not afraid of you."   /   "But he's the greatest sorcerer of our time."   /   "You can't catch me, I'm faster than the wind!"   /   "It'll never work, we need a new plan."
Model responses
"I'm not afraid of you," Jack said evenly, refusing to back away.
"But he's the greatest sorcerer of our time!" Marina protested, her voice cracking with disbelief.
"You can't catch me, I'm faster than the wind!" the boy yelled with a wild grin.
"It'll never work, we need a new plan," she muttered through gritted teeth.

What Is Happening

Same line. Add a different action around it — watch how the meaning shifts.

Alice knelt down and whispered, "If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now."
Tender, almost intimate. She has lowered herself to say this gently.
Alice's face went bright red as she turned and shouted at Paul, "If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now!"
Furious. The blushing face, the turn, the shout — we feel the heat.
Alice reached out and grabbed Paul's arm before hissing, "If it wasn't for you, we would all be safe by now!"
Threatening. The grab tells us she means it, even if the voice is low.

Try one yourself. Pick a line below. Add both how it is said and what is happening around it.

"It's cold out here isn't it?"   /   "Are you afraid?"   /   "You can't make me do that! I'll never agree to it."   /   "Who would have done such a thing?"
Model responses
"It's cold out here, isn't it?" muttered Fred as he shivered uncontrollably and shoved his hands deeper into his pockets.
Maya took a step back, her eyes wide. "Are you afraid?" she whispered.
"You can't make me do that!" Tom yelled, slamming his fist against the wall. "I'll never agree to it."

Scenario Writing

Pick a scenario. Write a few lines of dialogue between the two characters. Use what is said, how it is said, and what is happening.

Two friends making an evil plan to trick their teacher
A small child driving their parent mad with questions
One character telling another an unbelievable story
A mother arguing with her son about getting ready for school
Two girls arguing about their favourite sports
Two chefs discussing their favourite meals
Two robots planning their escape from human masters
Make up one of your own
Aim: at least six lines back and forth. Vary your "said" verbs. Slip in one or two short actions between speeches.
Model response (parent and child)
"Why is the sky blue?" Eli asked, tugging at his mum's sleeve as she tried to butter the toast.
"Because... it just is, sweetheart."
"But why?" he insisted, his head tilted up like a hopeful sunflower.
"Eli, please." His mum sighed deeply. "Eat your toast."
"But why is toast brown?"
"Because the bread cooks, Eli."
"And why does it cook?" he asked, already opening his mouth for the next question.

Write a Full Scene

Choose one of the narrative scenarios below. Rewrite it as a scene of dynamic dialogue.

Option A: Harry Potter and Professor Snape
After a Quidditch match, Snape stopped Harry, looking very serious. He seemed to question whether Harry had played fairly, making Harry feel a mix of surprise and annoyance. This talk happened in front of everyone, making students whisper about what was really going on between Harry and Snape.
Option B: Katniss and Peeta
In District 12, Peeta saw Katniss staring off into space, deep in thought. He walked over and asked her what was on her mind. At first, Katniss didn't want to say, but then she started talking about what scared her and what she hoped for their future.
Option C: Sherlock and Watson
After solving another mystery, Holmes and Watson sat in their living room, reflecting on the case. Holmes, with his pipe, made some clever remarks about the clues they found. Watson, amazed by Holmes' skill, agreed and added his own thoughts.
Option D: Moana and Maui
After they put the heart back into Te Fiti, Moana and Maui sat down to talk about everything they went through. Maui shared how he felt about their journey. Moana listened, feeling happy and a bit sad that their adventure was over.
Checklist before you start writing:
  • Quotation marks around the spoken words.
  • New paragraph each time the speaker changes.
  • A mix of "said" and stronger verbs.
  • At least two moments of "what is happening" around the words.
Model response (Option A)
"Potter." Snape's voice cut through the noise of the crowd like a knife. Harry froze.
"Sir?" He turned slowly, broomstick still clutched in his hand.
"A word." Snape's lip curled. "About your... technique."
"My technique?" Harry asked, trying to keep his voice level. "We won, sir."
"You did indeed." Snape replied softly, though his eyes glittered with something that wasn't quite congratulation. "Tell me, Potter — do you always rely on luck?"
Around them, the crowd had begun to murmur. A few first years hovered nearby, pretending not to listen.
"I rely on practice, sir," Harry said, his jaw tightening.

Notice the mix of verbs (cut through, curled, replied, glittered) and the way action is woven between the words. Compare with your version.
