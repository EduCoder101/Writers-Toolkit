Three Ways to EndClimax, resolution, and the last line

We are learning that a strong ending is earned: the main character solves the boulder herself, and the last few sentences leave the reader with a feeling.

25 minutesQuick practice lessonThree writing burstsReflective · Circular · Twist

Your sizzling start, pebble, rock and boulder are done. Today we climb to the top and come back down.

Success criteria
I can say who solves the boulder in a story: the main character, not somebody else.
I can name three kinds of ending, reflective, circular and twist, and tell them apart.
I can write the last four or five sentences of a story so the reader is left with a feeling.
1 · Hook3 minTalk

How did that feel?

A story has built and built. The boulder has arrived. Here come the last lines.

Read this aloud

The dragon opened its jaws. Fire roared towards Sophie. She screamed and closed her eyes… and then she woke up in bed. It was all a dream. The end.

Thumbs, then talkThumbs up if that ending landed. Thumbs down if it did not. Tell your partner what the writer just did to you.

The writer took the easy way out. The dragon never mattered, Sophie never had to be brave, and every worry you felt was for nothing. Readers feel cheated, and we never use these three:

×
The dream
…and then I woke up. It was all a dream.
Nothing that happened was real, so none of it counted.
×
The dinner
…and then we went home and had dinner.
The story just stops. No feeling, no echo, no surprise.
×
The swoop-in rescue
Then Mum arrived and fixed everything.
Somebody else solves the problem. The main character did nothing.
In your bookWrite the date and the title Three Ways to End. Underneath, copy: The main character solves the boulder herself. Never a dream, never dinner, never a rescue.

Climax, resolution and ending
Today

Climax: the peak, where the boulder is faced. Resolution: how she gets through it. Ending: the last four or five sentences.

Say and ask

Read Sophie's dragon in your most dramatic voice, then flatten completely for "It was all a dream." Let the groan happen. The class already knows the rule from earlier lessons, so keep this to three minutes: one quick talk, the three banned endings, write the rule, move on.

2 · I do5 minWatch and talk

Lily's climax and resolution

Lily is lost in the dark on camp with little Ava, and a storm has rolled in. That is her boulder. Watch how the peak of the story works, then see three ways the same story could end.

ClimaxThe peak. The boulder is faced.

Lightning lit up the bush. Ava screamed. I grabbed her hand. Think, Lily. Think. Then I remembered Mr Patel's voice at orientation: "If you're ever lost, follow the creek downhill. It runs right past the dining hall." I could hear it, rushing somewhere below us.

ResolutionHow she gets through it.

"Climb on," I said, crouching down. Ava wrapped her arms around my neck. Step by slippery step, I followed the sound of the water. My legs burned. Twice I nearly fell. Then, through the rain, a yellow light. The dining hall. "LILY!" Mr Patel was running towards us.

Talk to your partnerWho solved the problem? Mr Patel is in the story twice. Did he rescue Lily?
Learn thisLily solved it. She remembered, she decided, she carried Ava, she followed the creek. Mr Patel only appears after the hard part is done. The main character does the solving, and adults can arrive at the end.
Lily's story so far
  • Sizzling start: "My torch flickered twice, then died."
  • Pebble: walking back from the toilet block in the dark.
  • Rock: she takes the wrong track and gets lost. Little Ava is with her.
  • Boulder: the storm hits. Ava is crying. Nobody knows where they are.
Now the story needs its last lines. Here are three ways to end it.

Same story, same resolution. Only the last four or five sentences change. Which one do you like best?

Reflective
She thinks about what she learned.
Mr Patel wrapped us both in a blanket. Ava fell asleep against my shoulder before we even reached the cabins. I used to think being brave meant not being scared. Now I know it means being scared and doing it anyway.
What it does: steps back from the action and tells us what the character understands now that she did not before.
Circular
The ending echoes the sizzling start.
The next morning, Ava found me at breakfast. I pressed something into her hand: a brand-new torch from the camp shop, with fresh batteries. "Just in case," I said. She flicked it on. It did not flicker once.
What it does: the story began with a torch dying. It ends with a torch that works. The reader feels the circle close.
Twist
A small surprise that still makes sense.
On the last morning, Mr Patel called my name in front of everyone. He handed me a badge. It said Camp Leader. Next year, I would be the one the little ones followed through the dark.
What it does: something we did not expect, that fits everything that came before. Not a dragon, not a dream. A badge.
In your bookWrite the three names with a short definition for each: Reflective: what she learned Circular: echoes the start Twist: a small surprise that fits
Say and ask
  • Read the climax, pause, and ask what Lily is doing at the peak: thinking, remembering, deciding. Reveal the resolution and ask "Who solved it?" before you reveal the answer. Expect a few students to say Mr Patel; that is the point of the question.
  • Reveal the three endings one at a time and read each aloud. After all three, take a quick vote. There is no right answer. The aim is that students hear the same story end three different ways.
  • Point out that every ending still gives Lily the win. The swoop-in rescue is banned, but an adult arriving after the character has done the work is fine.
3 · We do3 minThumbs

Which is which?

A new story, a new climax and resolution. Three endings follow, unlabelled. Decide with your partner which is reflective, which is circular and which is a twist.

StartThe sizzling start.

The back gate was swinging in the wind. Grace stared at it. Then she stared at the empty spot on the lawn where Biscuit should have been.

Boulder

She found him at the end of the street, trotting straight towards the main road, tail up, as if it were the best day of his life. A truck was coming. He did not look. He never looked.

Climax

Grace opened her mouth to scream his name and stopped. Shouting made him run faster. Everyone knew that. Then she remembered the dinner whistle, the two notes Dad used every night at six, and she put her fingers to her lips and whistled them as loud as she could.

Resolution

Biscuit skidded. His ears went up. He spun around and came galloping back up the footpath so fast he nearly knocked her over, and she clipped the lead onto his collar with hands that would not stop shaking. The truck rumbled past behind them.

Partner challengeRead the three endings. Point to the one you think is the twist, then the circular one, then the reflective one.
Ending A
That night, Grace checked the back gate before dinner. Then she checked it again after dinner. Then once more before bed, just to be sure. It did not swing again.
CircularThe story began with the swinging gate. It ends with the gate shut, checked three times.
Ending B
Dad came out to see what the fuss was about. Biscuit sat, wagged, and dropped something at his feet. It was the sausage from last night's barbecue. He had not run away at all. He had been shopping.
TwistA small surprise that still fits. He really was heading somewhere, just not where she feared.
Ending C
Grace had always thought a gate was just a gate. Now she knew it was the only thing standing between Biscuit and the road. Some small jobs are not small at all.
ReflectiveShe steps back and tells us what she understands now.
4 · Writing burst 1 of 34 minYou write

The Volcano

Read the story. It stops right after the resolution. Your job: write the last four or five sentences.

Start

"Do not touch the red bottle," Priya said, for the third time that morning. Arjun grinned and backed away from the kitchen bench with his hands in the air.

Build

By ten o'clock her volcano stood on the hall table, painted brown and dusted with grey chalk, with the red bottle of vinegar tucked behind it. Two tables down, Ben's robot arm was waving at everyone who walked past.

Boulder

Then the judges arrived. Priya tipped the bicarb into the crater, lifted the red bottle, and poured. Nothing. Not a fizz. Not a bubble. She poured again. The judges waited. Somebody behind her giggled.

Climax

Priya sniffed the bottle. Water. Plain tap water. Her stomach dropped. Then, across the hall, she spotted the canteen hatch, and beside the chips and the salt, the big plastic bottle of vinegar. "One minute," she said, and ran.

Resolution

She was back in forty seconds, breathless, clutching the canteen's vinegar. She poured. The crater hissed, foamed, and spilled red lava over the side of the table and onto the head judge's shoes. The judge looked down. Then she laughed.

Your four or five sentences go here.
Your ending
In your bookWrite the title The Volcano, then the last four or five sentences of the story. Finish where the story finishes, not with a whole new scene.
04:00
Reflective

What did Priya learn about what to do when things go wrong? (Not about volcanoes. About herself.)

Circular

The story began with a red bottle and a grinning little brother. Could the last line come back to one of them?"Do not touch the red bottle."

Twist

Who is the one person who could have swapped the bottle? What might Priya find when she gets home? The twist still has to make sense.

  • Priya solved the problem herself. (She did. Keep it that way.)
  • Nobody woke up. Nobody went home for dinner.
  • Four or five sentences, not fifteen.
  • The last line leaves a feeling, an echo, or a surprise.
Running the burst
  • Read the story aloud once, quickly, with energy. Students write in their books. Keep the page projected so the story stays in front of them.
  • Start the timer. Students who have an idea write at once. For those who are stuck, press "Need an idea?" for one nudge per ending type, and open the word bank and sentence starters if the room needs them. Push them to pick one and go, not to read all three and freeze.
  • With 30 seconds left, say "last sentence". Take two volunteers, and ask the class to name the ending type each one used.
5 · Writing burst 2 of 34 minYou write

Twenty-Two Strokes

A second story, a second ending. Try a different type from the one you just used.

Start

"Take your marks." The starter's voice bounced off the water. I curled my toes over the edge of the block and stared at the far wall. Fifty metres. Two lengths. I had swum it a hundred times at training.

Boulder

The whistle went. I dived. The moment I hit the water, my goggles snapped. The strap flicked loose, the cold flooded in, and everything went to blur. I could not see the lane rope. I could not see the wall. I could not see anything.

Climax

For two strokes I did nothing but panic. Then I heard Coach Dani's voice in my head, the thing she said every single Tuesday: "Count. Twenty-two strokes to the wall. You know this." So I counted. Eight. Nine. Ten. My hand brushed the lane rope and I shoved away from it. Twenty. Twenty-one. Twenty-two. I stretched out my arm.

Resolution

Tiles. I flipped, pushed off, and counted again, all the way back. When my hand slapped the wall the second time, I ripped off the goggles and looked up at the board. Fourth. No ribbon. Mum was on her feet anyway, and so was Coach Dani.

Your ending
In your bookWrite the title Twenty-Two Strokes, then the last four or five sentences of the story. Finish where the story finishes, not with a whole new scene.

Reflective

Fourth place and no ribbon. What did the swimmer win instead? What does she understand now about racing, or about herself?

Circular

The story began with "Take your marks" and a wall she stared at. What could the last line echo? The block, the wall, the voice, the counting?"Take your marks."

Twist

Coach Dani is on her feet. What is she holding, or about to say, that the swimmer does not expect? It must fit everything that came before.

6 · Writing burst 3 of 34 minYou write

Nan's Cake

Last one. If you have used two types already, this is your chance to try the third.

Start

The cake slid. Zara watched it go: slowly at first, then all at once, three layers of chocolate sponge leaning like a tower and folding into a heap on the kitchen floor.

Build

It was Nan's eightieth birthday. Twenty people were arriving in an hour. Mum was at the airport collecting Uncle Sam, and Zara had promised, promised, that the cake would be ready.

Boulder

There was no more flour. There was no time to bake again anyway. The doorbell rang. The first guests were early.

Climax

Zara stared at the wreck on the floor. Then she looked at the bench. Cream. Strawberries. Chocolate sauce. The big glass bowl Nan used for Christmas trifle. She grabbed the biggest unbroken pieces of sponge, washed her hands, and started layering: sponge, cream, strawberries, sponge, cream, sauce.

Resolution

When Nan walked in, the glass bowl sat in the middle of the table with one big candle on top. Nan looked at it for a long moment. "A trifle," she said. "My mother used to make trifle." She hugged Zara so hard that her glasses went crooked.

Your four or five sentences go here.
Your ending
In your bookWrite the title Nan's Cake, then the last four or five sentences of the story. Finish where the story finishes, not with a whole new scene.
04:00
Reflective

Zara promised a cake and made something else. What did she learn about promises, or about mistakes, or about Nan?

Circular

The story began with a cake sliding to the floor. What is on the floor, or on the table, or in the bowl, at the very end?The cake slid.

Twist

What does Nan say or do next that nobody expects? What if the trifle, not the cake, is what the family asks for next year?

7 · Exit2 min

Label your three endings

1Next to each ending you wrote, put R, C or T. Did you use all three types, or did you keep going back to a favourite?

2Now open your own story plan. Look at your boulder. Which kind of ending will you choose for your story? Write the type next to Conclusion.

3Finish this sentence in your book: My ending will leave the reader feeling…

For the teacher

Look for: four or five sentences, not a whole new scene; the main character still has the win; the type is recognisable. A student who writes three reflective endings has understood one type well; nudge them to a circular ending in their own story. A twist that does not fit the story is the most common wobble, so ask "Does that make sense with everything before it?"
