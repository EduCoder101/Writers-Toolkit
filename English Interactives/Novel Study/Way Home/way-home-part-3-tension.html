Learning Intention

We are learning to analyse how language and illustrations build mood, tension and emotional responses.

Success Criteria

  • I can explain how specific language choices create mood and tension.
  • I can connect language and picture evidence to how the reader feels.

Before the lesson
  • Students need books and pencils. No printing.
  • Have the book ready to read the chase aloud.
  • Put students in pairs, then into four groups (about three pairs each) for card 5. The laminated chase, freeway and tree spreads from Part 2 suit groups A, B and D. Group C (the dog) uses the page in the book.
Vocabulary

Imagery, onomatopoeia, repetition, simile, metaphor, mood, pacing, connotation, atmosphere. Introduce each word as it comes up rather than all at once.

Suggested timing (about 50 minutes)

Mood change: 5 · Technique table: 15 · Precise words: 5 · Your passage: 18 · Exit slip: 5

Keep in mind

Accept different interpretations when students back them up with words from the text. Students do not need to find every technique in a passage: depth of explanation matters more than the number of labels.

Two turning points
“Uh oh” flips calm into fear. Then the tension climbs until the freeway, and drops when Shane is safe and laughing.
Read aloud
  • Start a few lines early, at “Here's a real nasty sight”, so students hear Shane trying to act cool. Read on through the chase to the freeway and “laughing and laughing”.
  • Students give a thumbs up when the mood changes. Ask: which word or phrase did it?
  • Reveal the mood line, then the turning points. Keep this short: the next card is where the writing starts.

Row 1 · I do
“Go! Go! Up the lane”
Repetition, short exclamations
Short commands speed up the pace, like Shane shouting “escape!” The reader feels the chase is happening right now.
Row 2 · We do
“a loud thump and a hard knock and a thud, thud, thud”
Onomatopoeia, repetition
We hear the running feet and the bumps inside the coat, so the speed and danger feel loud and close.
Row 3 · You do
“a wide shiny river of cars”
Metaphor
The traffic is a river: fast, never stopping and dangerous to cross. Shane's leap seems even riskier.
Row 4 · You do
“the big sea of city below”
Metaphor, sibilance
The city is huge, like a sea, and we look down on it from up high. The hissing s sounds feel a little threatening, like a snake.
Row 5 · You do
“Then up and up and up.”
Repetition joined by “and”
The climb goes on and on, stretching out the danger so we hold our breath. “Up” might also feel hopeful.

Learn this
Writers choose techniques to change how the reader feels, not just to decorate their writing.
How to run it
  • Row 1: think aloud and reveal. Students copy it.
  • Row 2: students suggest the technique and effect before you reveal. Build the sentence together.
  • Rows 3 to 5: students work in their books. Reveal each row only to check.
  • This sequence comes from last year's notes: modelling one row, doing one together, then letting students complete the rest led to rich discussion and new vocabulary.
Questions to ask
  • What does the repetition make the reader expect will happen next?
  • Why compare the traffic to a river rather than just describe the cars?
  • Row 5: some students may notice the additive conjunction “and” makes problems feel endless, or that “up” can feel hopeful. Praise both.
Technique bank meanings

Onomatopoeia: a word that sounds like its noise. Metaphor: says one thing is another. Simile: compares using “like” or “as”. Sibilance: repeated s sounds.

Four precise words

In your book
Write the four words. Next to each, write what you think it means. Use the sentence to help you.
forlorn
“Past a thin forlorn park.”
Means: sad, lonely and left behind.
Why “forlorn” and not “quiet”?
peers
“The boy called Shane peers down another long lane.”
Means: looks closely, searching.
What does it tell us about how Shane feels?
tindery
“…down the slippery trunk to the tindery fence.”
Means: dry and crumbly, easy to break, like tinder for a fire.
Why might that fence feel risky?
pussyfooting
“No more pussyfooting, Cat Number One. This time, we're going right on home.”
Means: moving slowly and carefully, trying not to be noticed.
Why is it a clever word in a story about a cat?
Run it
  • Give students two minutes to write their guesses first. Then reveal one word at a time and discuss its question.
  • Students tick the guesses they got close to and fix the others.
  • Pussyfooting: Shane says there will be no more creeping about or delaying; they are going straight home. The word also plays on “pussycat”.

In your book
  1. Copy your group's passage.
  2. Underline a strong verb, a sound or picture phrase and a repeated word or pattern.
  3. Pick two. Fill a table row for each: Evidence, Language choice, What it suggests, Effect on the reader.
  4. Look at the picture for your passage. Finish: The picture makes the mood ___ because ___.

While pairs work
  • Ask: “What does that word make you feel? Why that word and not a plainer one?”
  • Push for the link between technique and effect, e.g. “The verb ‘leaps’ shows sudden, daring movement, making Shane's crossing seem dangerous.”

Assessment check

Look for exact evidence, a named language choice and a clear effect on the reader. Depth of explanation matters more than naming many techniques. Accept any interpretation students can justify with the text.
