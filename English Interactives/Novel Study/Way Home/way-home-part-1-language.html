Learning Intention

We are learning to analyse how an author's language choices reveal character and shape the reader's feelings.

Success Criteria

  • I can identify significant language choices in Way Home.
  • I can explain how word choices shape the reader's understanding of Shane and the cat.
Before the lesson
  • Post the Way Home transcript to Google Classroom. Students need it open from card 4.
  • Have the book ready, but keep the pictures hidden today. Part 2 looks at Gregory Rogers' illustrations.
  • All student work goes in books. No printing needed.
  • The story follows a boy living rough in a city at night. Keep the discussion on Hathorn's language and on Shane's care for the cat.
Vocabulary for this lesson

Connotation, characterisation, action verbs, saying verbs, pronouns, modality, repetition, inference. Introduce each term as it comes up rather than front-loading the list.

Suggested timing (40 minutes)

Listen: 5 · Model verbs: 5 · Verb table: 10 · Names and pronouns: 5 · Names for the cat: 10 · Exit slip: 5. Go deeper runs alongside for early finishers.

Verbs that do the work

One well-chosen verb can tell us how a character feels. Try swapping the verb. What changes?

Action verb (doing)Saying verb
Evidencethe exact words
Analysiswhat the verb suggests
Effect on the readerwhat we feel or understand
“…this cat with no name scrambles up a fence.”
Action verb Scrambles suggests hurried, uncontrolled movement.
The reader senses the cat's fear and urgency.
“…the boy called Shane laughs”
Saying verb Laughs suggests amusement, not aggression, when he first meets the cat.
The reader begins to see Shane as playful and interested in the cat.
“He pushes the little catface right down into his jacket.”
Action verb Pushes suggests urgency and protection while they are being chased.
The reader understands that Shane is trying to keep the cat safe.
“…the boy called Shane yells as he dives through.”
Action verb Dives shows rapid, risky movement through the freeway traffic.
The reader feels the tension and sees the danger Shane will face for the cat.
Talk to your partner
Which verbs make Shane seem protective? Which verbs create tension?
Learn this
Verbs do more than describe actions. They help the reader infer how a character feels, what they want and what is happening to them.
How to run this card
  • Before revealing row 1, press Try “walks” and ask: how would the meaning change if Hathorn wrote walks instead of scrambles?
  • On row 4, press Try “crosses” and ask: why might dives be more effective than crosses?
  • Link back to earlier mentor texts and the work on action (doing) verbs.
  • Likely answers. Protective: pushes (also strokes, reaches out slowly). Tension: scrambles, dives through, runs hard.

Names and pronouns tell us about relationships. Talk about each phrase with your partner.

Learn this
Names and pronouns show relationships. Repeated labels keep characters apart. Pronouns like we and us show them growing closer.
Steer the talk
  • The repeated descriptions first set the two up as individuals who do not know each other. The narrator keeps using both labels to the very end.
  • “The boy called Shane” introduces him as if we are meeting a stranger, and keeps a little distance between him and the reader.
  • We includes Shane and the cat. Listen for how often Shane says we, us and our (“our place”, “our Jag”) as the story goes on.
  • You'n me pairs them as equals, squeezed into one sound: companions.

Names in the transcript

Scaredycat, Wildcat, Spitfire, Kitten Number One, Bestcat, Catlegs, Cat, Mycat, Animal, Kittycat, Whiskettes, Hungry, Skinny Minny, Upcat, Blackeyes, pussycat, Crazycat, Catseyes, Cat Number One, Noname.

Possible answers
  • The many names show intimacy and connection as the boy tries to survive.
  • They could also suggest chaos and change: Shane never knows from one day to the next whether things will stay the same.
  • Many names fit the moment (Spitfire when it spits, Hungry outside the restaurant, Upcat in the tree), so Shane is paying close attention to the cat. The last one is Noname.

Extra challenge

Compare how Shane talks to the cat at the start of the story with how he talks at the end. How does his language change? What does this show about their relationship?

Modality

If students choose “I'd break my neck for you”, praise what it shows: an emphatic willingness to take risks for the cat. Make the distinction that this is strong commitment, not a straightforward example of high grammatical modality.

Extra challenge pointers

At the start Shane yells and teases (“Scaredycat!”). By the end he reassures and comforts, and the pronouns are all shared: we, our place, home.
