Learning Intention

We are learning to explain how illustrations communicate ideas and shape the reader's interpretation of a story.

Success Criteria

  • I can use the words salience, contrast, composition, visual perspective, symbolism, mood, gutter and inference.
  • I can identify visual choices and back up what I notice with evidence from the picture.
  • I can explain how the illustrations and the words work together to influence the reader.

Suggested timing

Lesson B (about 50 minutes): recall 4 · model 7 · words and pictures 6 · detectives 20 · share 8 · exit slip 5.

Handle with care

The words never say it, but the pictures let readers infer that Shane has no house and sleeps rough. Some students may have experience of housing insecurity. Keep the focus on how the author and illustrator build understanding, and on Shane's strength, humour and care for the cat, rather than pity. Hathorn dedicates the book to "the largely unsung, mostly unseen workers for young people in need".

Observation or interpretation?

Students should separate what they can see from what they work out. "The window separates Shane from the room" is an observation. "The window symbolises exclusion" is an interpretation that needs justifying with evidence.

When an object stands for a bigger idea. A heart can stand for love, a dove for peace, a broken chain for freedom, a lantern for hope.

Say

"A symbol is an interpretation, so it needs evidence. What can you see that makes the window feel like a barrier?" (The glass, the railings, Shane below and outside, the cat above and inside.)

Other possible symbols in the book: the torn black edges on every page (a rough, torn life?), and the posters of reaching hands on the alley wall (connection?). Accept any reading students can back up.

TalkIn your book

What happened in between?

Talk to your partner

Learn this
The gutter
The gap between two pictures, or the fold down the middle of a double page. Readers fill the gap using inference.
Add row 8 to your toolkit
Gutter, what it means, and a sketch of two boxes with a gap between them.
Gregory Rogers, Way Home
Find it in Way Home
Find Shane and find the cat. Which side of the gutter is each one on?
One reading
Shane is on the left page and the cat is on the right page, with the gutter between them: two strangers. A few pages later, the cat is tucked inside Shane's jacket, on the same page as him.
Find it in Way Home
Say

"In comics, the gutter is the white space between panels. In a picture book, the gutter is also the fold in the middle. Illustrators think about what goes on each side of it."

Use the magnifier to find the cat peering over the top of the fence, right of the fold. The gutter keeps the two characters apart at the start of the story.

Link back to tool 1: whenever we fill a gap, we are inferring.

SalienceWhat grabs your attention first?
The huge golden window, then a small dark figure: Shane.
Our eyes follow Shane's eyes, so we notice what he wants.
Colour and contrastWhich parts are light or dark?
Inside is bright, warm and golden. Shane stands in grey shadow outside.
The contrast shows the gap between comfort inside and Shane's life outside.
CompositionWhere is Shane placed? How big is he?
Shane is tiny, at the bottom, under giant stone arches and columns.
He seems small and alone in a huge city.
SymbolismWhat might the window stand for?
His hand is pressed flat against the glass.
The window could be a barrier: he can see the good things inside but cannot reach them.

We stand behind Shane and look past him at the window.
We see what Shane sees, so we start to share his feelings.
Think aloud
  • Before each reveal, ask the question in the first column and take two or three ideas. Then reveal and compare.
  • Stress the order: point to the evidence first (I see), then say what it might mean (I think). Interpretations must be supported by what is actually visible.
  • Remind students of their first look in Lesson A. The toolkit has turned a first impression into an explanation.
Lesson B · We do
TalkIn your book

Now add the words

Gregory Rogers, Way Home
“Take a look at that Fatcat will you? I'm telling you that cat's a loser. Eats fancy mince, no kidding. Heaps of it. Right there at the window. Disgusting. And get that collar. What a joke!”Libby Hathorn, Way Home
1What do Shane's words show about how he feels about the cat inside?
2What does the picture show that the words do not say?
3Together, do the words and picture make you laugh, feel sorry, or something else?
4Why might Shane notice the cat's food and collar? What might that suggest about his own life?
Learn this
Words and pictures can work together in three ways.
Reinforcesay the same thing
Extendadd something new
Complicateseem to disagree
In your book
Copy the three words and their meanings. Then finish this: On the Fatcat page, the picture ___ the words because ___.
Possible answers
  • 1. He sounds scornful and tough: "loser", "Disgusting", "What a joke!"
  • 2. The cat sits high up, warm and golden behind glass. Shane stands below, outside the railings, beside rubbish bags, in the dark.
  • 3. His words are funny, but the picture can make us feel for him. He might be jealous.
  • 4. He notices food and a collar, so he may be hungry and may not belong to anyone. The picture complicates his tough words: it lets us infer what he does not say.

Check your sentences

A strong example (the cover)

The illustration communicates how small and alone Shane is in the city. The artist achieves this through composition: Shane is tiny under the huge arches. The words "Six shiny sportscars, all in a row" show what he wishes for, encouraging the reader to understand his longing.

Assessment check

Look for: a named visual choice, evidence that is actually visible, an effect on the reader, and a link to the words. Note students who describe the picture but do not explain its effect.
