Who designed our flag, and who was left out?

Every flag is an argument. Somebody chose its colours, its symbols and its stories, and by choosing, they also left things out. In these three lessons you will investigate the flag we have, design the flag you think Australia deserves, and then give your flag a voice in a poem.

Part 1 · Close reading Reference · no writing needed

Read the flag like a text

A flag can be read the way you read a picture book: every element was placed on purpose, by an author, for an audience. Match the numbers on the flag to the notes beside it.

  • 1
    The Union JackThe flag of the United Kingdom, placed in the position of honour. It tells the story of Australia’s British colonial history, and of loyalty to Britain in 1901.
  • 2
    The Commonwealth StarSeven points: one for each of the six states, and one for all of Australia’s territories. It began with six points in 1901 and gained a seventh in 1908.
  • 3
    The Southern CrossA constellation seen in the southern sky. It says we are a land of the south. First Nations peoples have told stories about these same stars for tens of thousands of years.

? Did you know?

The Aboriginal Flag and the Torres Strait Islander Flag, shown at the top of this page, have both been official flags of Australia since 1995. They fly beside the national flag, but they are not part of it.

Q10

The designer’s toolbox Read closely · you will use this in Part 2

A flag speaks without words

Flags cannot write sentences, so they speak in colour and symbol. Around the world, designers have built a shared visual language. Learn it here, then use it to make your own flag say something true.

What colours can say

RedCourage, sacrifice, the earth and ochre of this continent, lifeblood and kinship.
Yellow & goldThe sun, the giver of life, golden wattle, beaches and harvest.
BlueSky and sea, the oceans that surround us and that most Australians crossed to get here.
GreenThe land itself, forests, growth, hope and new beginnings.
BlackOn the Aboriginal Flag, black represents Aboriginal people. It can also stand for strength and determination.
WhitePeace and honesty. On the Torres Strait Islander Flag, white stands for peace.
OchreThe colours of country: desert sands, rock and the oldest continuing cultures on Earth.
Many coloursDiversity: many peoples, languages and stories sharing one place.

What symbols can say

StarsGuidance, hope, and finding your way. The Southern Cross appears on the flags of Australia, New Zealand, Papua New Guinea, Samoa and Brazil.
The sunLife, energy and new dawns. The yellow circle at the heart of the Aboriginal Flag is the sun, the constant renewer of life.
CirclesUnity, meeting places and belonging. A circle has no first place and no last place: everyone inside it is equal.
WavesThe sea, and journeys across it: ancient voyages, tall ships, and the boats and planes that still carry new Australians here.
PlantsGolden wattle is Australia’s national flower, blooming even after fire. Plants can say growth, survival and care for country.
Paths & journeysFootsteps, songlines and roads: where a people have been, and where they choose to go next, together.

Real flags, real choices

See the language of colour and symbol at work around the world. Two of these countries faced exactly the question you are facing, and chose a new flag.

Canada, 1965 Until 1965, Canada’s flag carried the Union Jack. After a fierce national debate, Canadians chose a single maple leaf: a symbol from the land itself, belonging to no one group and to everyone.
South Africa, 1994 Adopted when apartheid ended. The sideways Y is read as different paths coming together into one road ahead, and the six colours gather up the flags of South Africa’s many peoples.
Japan One red circle: the rising sun, for the land of the sunrise. Proof that a single well-chosen symbol can carry a whole country’s identity, with nothing else needed.
Greenland, 1985 The sun rising over the ice, drawn with just two colours swapped over. Greenlanders chose this over a Nordic cross design: their own symbol, not a borrowed one.
Seychelles, 1996 Five rays bursting out of the corner: a young country moving into its future. Blue for sky and sea, yellow for the sun, red for the people, white for justice, green for the land.
Bangladesh, 1971 A red sun rising over a green land, remembering those who died for independence. The circle sits slightly towards the flagpole so it looks centred when the flag is flying.

Countries really do change their flags

Canada changed in 1965. South Africa changed in 1994. New Zealand held a national vote in 2016 and chose to keep its flag. Changing a flag, or keeping it, is always a decision made by people, which means it is a decision you are allowed to have an opinion about.

Read two flags as mentor texts

On the Aboriginal Flag, Harold Thomas used just three colours: black for Aboriginal people, red for the earth and their spiritual relationship to it, and yellow for the sun. On the Torres Strait Islander Flag, Bernard Namok used green for the land, blue for the sea, black for the people, a white dhari (a dancer’s headdress) for Torres Strait Islanders, and a white five-pointed star for the island groups, peace and navigation. Notice how every single element earns its place. That is your standard in Part 2.

Part 2 · Design Brainstorm & drafts in your English book

Your commission: a new flag for Australia

You have been asked to design a new Australian flag that reflects an inclusive national identity: a flag with room in it for everyone who calls this country home. Real flag designers follow a few rules that keep flags strong. So will you.

Weak flags usually…

  • cram in ten symbols so no single one matters
  • use six or more colours that fight each other
  • rely on words or names (a flag is not a poster)
  • copy another country’s flag too closely
  • use symbols the designer cannot explain

Strong flags usually…

  • are so simple a child could draw them from memory
  • use two or three colours that mean something
  • let every symbol carry a story
  • look like nowhere else on Earth
  • still work when small, far away, or flapping in wind

Design it in five steps

  1. 1. Brainstorm List the people, places, ideas and stories your flag must honour. Aim for at least ten. Circle the three that matter most to you. English book
  2. 2. Choose your symbols For each circled idea, sketch two or three symbols that could carry it. Steal wisely from the designer’s toolbox above. English book
  3. 3. Choose your colours Pick two or three colours and write one sentence saying what each colour means on your flag. English book
  4. 4. Draft three rough flags Small, quick rectangles. Squint at them from arm’s length: which one still speaks? English book
  5. 5. Draw your final flag Take your best draft to the paper your teacher gives you. Use the full space, press your colours in solidly, and keep your edges clean. Paper from your teacher
The memory test When you think you are finished, show a classmate your flag for five seconds, then hide it and ask them to describe it. If they can, your design is strong. If they can’t, simplify.

Explain your thinking

Every new flag is accompanied by a designer’s explanation of the symbols, colours and design choices. Draft yours in your English book using all four starters, then write a neat copy to display beside your flag.

  1. My flag includes ______ because…
  2. This symbol represents…
  3. I chose these colours to show…
  4. I was inspired by the idea that…

Part 3 · Write Draft in your English book

If my flag could speak…

Imagine your newly designed flag could speak. What would it say to the people of Australia? What story would it tell about identity, fairness and belonging? Use the “I am” structure from earlier in the unit. Here is a model to learn from, not to copy.

Model poem
I am a flag shaped by voices you don’t always hear,
I feel the footsteps of those who came before,
I see a land held by generations,
I hold the colours of truth and hope.
I worry I’ll be ignored like the ones I speak for,
I hope I fly in peace, not in pride,
I wish to unite, not divide,
I am more than a symbol, I am a choice.

Read the model like a writer

The highlighted words are the “I am” skeleton: I am, I feel, I see, I hold, I worry, I hope, I wish, I am. The first and last lines match on purpose, and the last line lands the message. Notice the abstract nouns (truth, hope, peace, pride) and the metaphor: the flag is a choice, not just cloth.

✓ Your poem must…

  • Use your own flag design as its inspiration
  • Include at least two symbols or colours from your flag
  • Use at least two abstract nouns and one metaphor
  • Be drafted in your English book first, then published neatly to display beside your flag

Metaphor starters

A metaphor says one thing is another. No like or as allowed.

Part 3 · Publish Canva

Publish in Canva

Once your teacher has seen your draft, publish your poem in Canva so it can be displayed beside your flag.

  1. 1. Edit first, on paper Read your draft aloud in a whisper. Fix anything that trips your tongue, then check spelling and punctuation. English book
  2. 2. Open Canva and choose a simple layout A poster or A4 document works well. Give your poem a title. Your flag’s name makes a good one. Canva
  3. 3. Type your poem carefully One line of the poem per line on the page. Choose one or two fonts at most, and colours drawn from your flag. Canva
  4. 4. Check, then submit Compare your published poem to your draft word by word, then share it with your teacher. Canva
