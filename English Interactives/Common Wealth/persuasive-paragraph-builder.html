Learning Intention & Success Criteria ▼

Learning Intention

We are learning to organise persuasive sentences into paragraphs so that each paragraph develops one clear argument.

Success Criteria

  • I can explain what makes a rewritten sentence stronger than the sloppy original.
  • I can work out the main argument a sentence is making.
  • I can place a sentence in the paragraph whose argument it supports.
  • I can order the sentences inside a paragraph so the argument builds from the topic sentence.
  • I can set aside a sentence that does not earn its place, and say why.

Go Deeper: does the order inside a paragraph matter?

You have decided which paragraph each sentence belongs to. That is the easy half. These three tasks investigate what happens inside a paragraph, where the order really does change how convincing the writing sounds.

Task 1. Move the topic sentence

Look at Paragraph 2 on your plan. Read it aloud in order. Now imagine the topic sentence moved to the very end, after all the other sentences. Read it aloud again.

Write what changes. Does the paragraph still make sense? Does it feel more or less convincing, and at what exact moment does the difference show up?

Model response

My first thought was that it would not matter much, because all the sentences are still there. When I read it aloud I was wrong. With the topic sentence at the end, the first two sentences felt like they were floating. I did not know what they were arguing for until the last line, so I was holding the ideas without a box to put them in. Moving it back to the front fixed that instantly. The only version that also worked was when I left a rhetorical question at the very end, because a question is not trying to explain the paragraph, it is trying to finish it.

Specialising. You tried one specific case, read it aloud, and used what actually happened to test an idea rather than guessing from the page.

Task 2. Where do rhetorical questions belong?

Four of the sentences in the bank are rhetorical questions. Find where you put them. Here is a claim: a rhetorical question is always strongest at the end of a paragraph.

Test the claim. Move one rhetorical question to the start of its paragraph and read it aloud. Then write your own claim about where rhetorical questions work best, in a sentence beginning ‘A rhetorical question works best when...’

Model response

A rhetorical question works best when the reader already has enough information to answer it in their head. At the start of Paragraph 4, ‘Why would we keep an anthem that leaves anyone out?’ felt pushy, because I had not yet been told that anyone was left out. At the end of the same paragraph it felt like a trap closing, because by then I agreed. So the claim is nearly right, but not because the end is magic. The end is just usually the place where the reader knows enough.

Conjecturing. You tested somebody else’s claim, found the part that held up and the part that did not, and replaced it with a sharper claim of your own.

Task 3. The sentence that fits everywhere

Sentence 18, ‘What most Australians ask for is simple: an anthem that treats every citizen with respect’, would sit reasonably in three of the four paragraphs.

Is a sentence that fits almost anywhere a strength or a weakness in a persuasive text? Take a position, then rewrite the sentence so that it could only belong in one paragraph, and explain which words did the work.

Model response

It is a weakness. A sentence that fits everywhere is not doing a job anywhere, because it does not add anything the paragraph did not already have. To lock it into Paragraph 4 I wrote: ‘In survey after survey, Australians have asked for the same simple thing: an anthem that treats every citizen with respect.’ The words ‘in survey after survey’ do the work. They turn a general feeling into evidence about what the public has said, and evidence about the public is the only thing Paragraph 4 is interested in. If I had written ‘an anthem that names the people who were here first’ instead, the same sentence would have locked into Paragraph 1.

Justifying. You took a position, proved it by changing the sentence, and named the exact words that changed where it belongs. That is the difference between an opinion and an argument.
