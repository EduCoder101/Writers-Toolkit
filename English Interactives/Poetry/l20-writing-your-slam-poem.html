Lesson 20 · Writing Your Slam Poem

Learning Intention & Success Criteria

Learning Intention

We are learning to plan and draft a slam poem that turns one small personal moment into powerful spoken words.

Success Criteria

  • I can choose a topic that is one specific moment, not a broad idea.
  • I can collect sensory details that show my moment instead of explaining it.
  • I can draft 8 to 12 lines that include a powerful image and some repetition.
  • I can give a peer specific feedback and use feedback to improve my own performance.

The rule: go small

Every poet who has ever bored an audience started with a topic that was too big.

Too big"I am going to write about world peace."
Still too big"I am going to write about my family."
Now we are getting somewhere"I am going to write about the afternoon my nan taught me to make scones and my hands were too slow."

Nobody can picture world peace. Everybody can picture flour on a bench. A slam poem does not need a big idea, it needs one tiny snapshot that you own.

Small is not the opposite of important. Small is how important gets in.

The funnel

Watch a big soggy topic get squeezed into something you could actually write.

1. Big themeFamily
2. One person or one placeMy nan's kitchen
3. One momentThe afternoon she taught me to make scones and my hands were too slow
4. Three things I could see, hear or touchFlour on the bench like frost · the radio she never turns off · her thumb pressing the edge of the dough

And there is your opening line, hiding in the details: Her hands were faster than the radio.

Show it, don't tell it

Telling names the feeling and stops. Showing hands the feeling to the audience and lets them have it themselves. Pick one.

Choose a flat, boring sentence and watch it get its body back.

What your poem needs

At least one metaphor or powerful image. One line where you say a thing is another thing, or paint a picture so clear the audience can see it.

Repetition or a rhythmic phrase. A line or a few words that come back. Repetition is how a slam poem gets a heartbeat.

Stressed and unstressed words. Read your draft aloud. Where does your voice push? If it pushes nowhere, the line is flat.

8 to 12 lines. More if you have more to say. Fewer only if every single line is doing work.

Now write it

First draft, in your English book or typed in Word or Docs. Use your planning sheet, not your memory.

  • Start in the middle of the moment, not at the beginning of the day.
  • Write it the way you would say it. If you would not say it out loud, cut it.
  • Leave the ending until last. It usually arrives once the rest exists.
  • Cross out is fine. Slam poems are drafted out loud and messy.

Stuck on line one?

  • Start with one of your three details.
  • Start with something somebody said.
  • Start with "The day that..."
  • Start with the thing you noticed last.

Read it to somebody

In pairs or threes. Read your draft out loud twice: once so they hear it, once so they can listen properly.

One part that really made me feel something was...

A line you could perform more strongly is...

Your message made me think about...

"It was good" is not feedback. Name the line.

Mark up your script

Rehearsing is not just reading it again. Take a pen to your draft and tell your voice what to do.

/Slash for a pauseOne slash, one beat of silence. Two slashes, make them wait.
__Underline to pushThe word your voice leans on. Two or three per line, no more.
↗Arrow for pitchUp to question or lift, down to land or accuse.

Then rehearse for

  • Pace, rhythm and pauses. Slower than feels natural. Nerves speed everybody up.
  • Intonation and pitch. If every line ends the same way, the poem sounds like a list.
  • Volume and eye contact. Look up at least twice. Find one friendly face.

Performance checklist

Rehearse at home, out loud, at least three times. Then check yourself against this.

  • Did I pause?
  • Did I make eye contact?
  • Did I change my tone?
  • Did I slow down enough for the audience to keep up?
  • Did my last line land, or did it trail off?

The rehearsal trick

  • Read it to a mirror once.
  • Read it to a person once.
  • Read it to a pet, a photo or an empty chair once.
  • The third one is where you stop performing and start meaning it.

Teacher notes

Not part of the lesson sequence. This panel only opens when you open it.

Before you start the deck

  • Say out loud that nobody has to write about anything they do not want to share, and that choosing a lighter topic is not choosing an easier one.
  • Say what will happen to the finished poems: who hears them, whether performing is expected, and what the alternative is. Students write more honestly when they know the audience in advance.
  • Offer the opt-outs now rather than later: read seated, read to you alone, read a section rather than the whole poem, or have a friend read it.

If a poem goes somewhere heavy

  • Personal writing sometimes surfaces things. Respond to the writing warmly and without alarm, then follow up privately rather than in front of the class.
  • Do not promise confidentiality before you know what you are being told.
  • If anything in a poem raises a concern about a child's safety or wellbeing, follow your school's child protection reporting process and talk to your school's designated person. That process, not this page, is the authority. Keep the poem.
  • You are the teacher here, not the counsellor. Passing it on quickly is the right move, not an overreaction.

Steering the deck

  • The prompts are deliberately concrete and everyday. If a student picks one and takes it somewhere heavier, that is their call, not a fault in the prompt.
  • Watch for the student who stars nothing. Usually it means the deck is being read as homework rather than as options. Shuffle a few with them and react out loud yourself.
  • Watch for the student who stars everything. Push them to one, then run the funnel with them at the board.

Where the funnel usually fails

  • Box 3 comes back as a period of time ("when I was little") rather than a moment. Ask: what were you actually doing? What could you hear?
  • Box 4 comes back as feelings. Send them back for things. Feelings belong in the poem, not in the plan.
  • A student stuck at box 1 usually does not need a smaller theme, they need a person. Skip to box 2 and come back.

Timing

  • Cards 1 to 5 run as one teaching block, about 25 minutes. Cards 6 and 7 launch the drafting.
  • Card 8 (peer feedback) is a natural stopping point. Cards 9 and 10 work as the start of the following session, or as the homework brief.
