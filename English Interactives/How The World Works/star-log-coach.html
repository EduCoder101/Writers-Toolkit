Good vs not yet

Two ways to open the same kind of story. One could be about any planet in any story. The other could only be about a real object in space. Press the button to see exactly what makes the difference.

Not yet

One day a girl landed on a strange planet. It was really scary and full of green aliens. They chased her, so she ran back to her spaceship and flew home. The end.

  1. 1“A strange planet” could be anywhere. There is nothing real to picture.
  2. 2“Really scary” tells us the feeling. Show it instead.
  3. 3Generic aliens, with no real science to ground the story.
  4. 4A rushed ending, with no feeling and nothing changed.
Strong

Far from the Sun, Commander Ito eased the probe onto Europa, a moon wrapped in cracked white ice. The ground glowed faintly in the starlight, and far below her boots lay a hidden ocean, deeper than any on Earth. “Mission Control,” she whispered, “the cracks are moving.”

  1. 1Adverbial phrase. “Far from the Sun” tells us where, and drops us straight into the scene.
  2. 2Appositive + a real feature. The commas add a true detail about the real moon.
  3. 3Sensory detail. We can see the glow, not just be told it is there.
  4. 4A real fact woven in. Europa really does hide an ocean beneath its ice.
  5. 5Ends on a hook. Now we have to keep reading.
Kind reminder: “not yet” just means it is on its way. Every fix is shown.

Good notes vs copying

Research is not copying. It is finding a fact, understanding it, and writing it in your own words so you can use it later. Here is the same fact, gathered two ways.

Not yet

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun and is often described as the ‘Red Planet’ because of the reddish appearance of its surface, which is caused by iron oxide, more commonly known as rust.

  1. 1Copied word for word from a website.
  2. 2One long sentence — that is not notes you can use.
  3. 3No source written down, so it cannot go in the bibliography.
Strong

Mars — 4th planet from the Sun Looks red: rusty iron in the soil Source: NASA Space Place

  1. 1Your own words. Short and clear, so you really understand it.
  2. 2Dot points. Easy to turn into report sentences later.
  3. 3Source noted. Ready for your bibliography.
Tip: read the fact, look away, then write it without peeking.

Report openings — good vs not yet

Your introduction is the first thing the reader meets. A flat one lists the obvious. A strong one hooks the reader and previews what is coming.

Not yet

My object is the Sun. This report is about the Sun. The Sun is a star. I will tell you some facts about the Sun.

  1. 1It repeats “the Sun” and “report” again and again.
  2. 2No hook — nothing makes us want to read on.
  3. 3It says what you will do, instead of just starting.
Strong

The Sun is the closest star to Earth, a giant ball of glowing gas so wide that a million Earths could fit inside it. Without its light and heat, nothing on our planet could survive. This report explains what the Sun is, how big it is, and why it matters.

  1. 1Classifies it. Tells the reader what kind of thing it is.
  2. 2A hook with a real number. A fact big enough to make us read on.
  3. 3Previews the report. Now we know what is coming.

Show, don’t tell

Telling names a feeling. Showing paints a picture and lets the reader feel it. Tap each one to turn a told line into a shown one.

Flat fact
The black hole was scary.
Stronger
The black hole swallowed every light, and gave nothing back.
Shown through what it does.
Flat fact
The nebula was beautiful.
Stronger
The nebula spilled pink and gold across the cold black sky.
Shown with colour and the senses.
Flat fact
Space felt lonely.
Stronger
One small light drifted on, with a million miles of dark on every side.
Shown with an image, not the word.
Flat fact
The old star was dying.
Stronger
Ring by glowing ring, the tired star let go of its skin.
Shown with an action.
Take it apart

Two tools: symbol and image

Your two mentor poems show two tools. Borrow both: paint your object with images, and let it carry a deeper feeling.

Symbol like Emily Dickinson

In ‘Hope is the thing with feathers’, Dickinson lets a small bird stand for hope. You can do the same: let your object carry a bigger feeling. A lone star might stand for hope; a black hole for the unknown; a nebula for a new beginning.

Image like Dorothea Mackellar

In ‘My Country’, Mackellar paints the land so clearly you can almost feel its heat and see its colours. Paint your object with the senses: “the comet streaked past, trailing ice and silver light.”
