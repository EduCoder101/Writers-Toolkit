A boy who wanted to fix ears

Graeme Clark was born on 16 August 1935 in Camden, a small town south-west of Sydney. His father, Colin, ran the local pharmacy and was badly deaf. Graeme watched customers shout their private questions across the counter and saw how embarrassed his father felt. When he was about ten, a visitor asked what he wanted to be when he grew up. "I want to fix ears," he answered.

In 1957 Graeme graduated in medicine from the University of Sydney and trained as an ear, nose and throat surgeon. In 1961 he married Margaret, a nurse, and they later had five children. He operated on ears for years, but nothing a surgeon could do helped people whose hearing nerve had died.

A crazy idea

In 1966 Clark gave up his surgical career to start a research degree. His question sounded impossible: could electricity, delivered straight to the hearing nerve, make a deaf person hear? In 1970 he became the first Professor of Otolaryngology at the University of Melbourne and set up a tiny laboratory. Many experts said the idea would never work. Money was so short that in 1974 a Channel 10 telethon raised funds so the work could continue.

The breakthrough came on a family holiday in 1977. Sitting on the sand at Minnamurra Beach, Clark picked up a spiral turban shell and pushed a blade of grass into its curling tunnel. The grass bent and slid in easily. He realised a bundle of thin, flexible wires could be guided around the spiral cochlea inside the ear in just the same way.

The first patient

On 1 August 1978, at the Royal Victorian Eye and Ear Hospital, Clark's team implanted the first multi-channel bionic ear into Rod Saunders, who had lost his hearing in a car accident. Weeks later, when the device was switched on and Rod heard the first notes of "God Save the Queen", Clark walked out of the room and wept. In 1979 a second patient, George Watson, received an implant and was soon chatting on the telephone.

Out into the world

In 1981 the Australian government backed a company, Cochlear, to manufacture the device. In 1984 Clark founded the Bionic Ear Institute to keep improving it. In 1985 the United States approved the bionic ear for adults, and in 1990 it was approved for children as young as two, which changed the lives of thousands of families. Clark received the Prime Minister's Prize for Science in 2004 and, in 2013, America's Lasker Award, often called the "American Nobel". Today more than half a million people around the world hear with a cochlear implant, and the turban shell sits in a museum.

The Bionic Ear which events serve which purpose

The text holds sixteen datable events. The grid shows the events a strong reworked timeline would be likely to include for each purpose. It is a guide for the discussion, not a marking key: a student who picks an event outside the ticks and argues for it convincingly has done the task well. The point to draw out is that the same event earns its place for different reasons (Rod Saunders belongs on every timeline, but for three different reasons), and that some events only belong on one.

Running it. Do not mention purpose at the start. Read the text together, then set Step 1 as a plain instruction: "make a timeline of eight events". Let them struggle with the choosing. Then stop the class and compare: put three or four very different timelines on the board and ask why they differ. Someone will say "it depends what it's for", and that is the moment to name purpose. Assign a purpose per table (or let them choose) and set Step 3. The three purposes on the sheet are deliberately different enough that the reworked timelines should share only a few events.

Discussion prompts. Which event appeared on every timeline in the room? Which event appeared on only one? Could a timeline with the marriage on it still be about how the device was built? What would a timeline look like if its purpose was "to persuade the government to fund medical research"?
